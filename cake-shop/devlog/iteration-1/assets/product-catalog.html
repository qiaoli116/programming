The business: what the bakery sells

Cake Order Desk · iteration 1 · devlog 20261113-03-product-catalog · all names, prices and designs are fictional

Before we design the system, we define the business. A bakery menu answers four questions. Why is the customer buying? That is the occasion. What kind of cake suits them, and how big? That is the product line and its size. Which design do they want? That is the template. What can they change? Those are the options. The answers to these questions are the design of one order.

1. The structure: four layers and one tag

The occasion is a tag, not a layer. One template can suit several occasions: a message mini cake works for a birthday, an anniversary or a thank-you. The occasion drives the suggestions, and the product line drives the rules. Notice, sizes, dietary limits and base prices come from how a cake is made, not from why it is bought.

2. Product lines: from small to big

Six kinds of cake. Every size says how many people it serves (party portions), so the assistant can turn "about 20 kids" into the sizes that fit across all lines.

Servings ruler: which line fits how many people

Each dot is one size and its price. For 20 guests, look at the dashed line: a 10" round, a 2-digit number cake, a half sheet and a box of 24 cupcakes all fit. Tiered cakes start at 30.

3. The menu: occasions × product lines

Each row is an occasion, each column a product line, and each chip a template. A template can appear in several rows. Click a chip to see what is fixed and what the customer can change. Dashed chips are seasonal.

4. The option library: one shared list

All customizations come from one list. Each product line says which option groups apply, and each template may fix or narrow them further. Every value has a price, so the price of any design can be added up from the catalog.

Price of a design = base price of the line and size + each chosen option + extras. Flat prices for iteration 1 (open question Q2: should they scale with size?).

5. Rules, four levels

These rules keep every design makeable. In the system, the Design checker applies all four levels to every design the AI proposes.

1 · Shop-wide: never
  • 3D sculpted shapes, likenesses of real people (sculpted or printed), more than 3 tiers
  • alcohol in the cake, delivery outside the local area, less than 2 days' notice
2 · Product line
  • sizes and minimum notice (tiered: 7 days, 3 tiers: 14 days)
  • which option groups apply; dietary limits (tiered cakes can't be gluten-free or vegan)
3 · Template
  • fixed parts can't be removed (a Unicorn round always has the unicorn topper)
  • a narrowed palette or sizes; a seasonal date window (Snowy Christmas: pickups 1–24 Dec)
4 · Option compatibility
  • red velvet and carrot can't be gluten-free or vegan; vegan only with vanilla or chocolate sponge
  • fondant only on round and tiered; printed picture only on sheet and round; at most 2 decorations (1 on cupcakes and minis)

6. Try it: find designs for an order

This is the first thing the Design assistant does with a new customer: work out the occasion, how many people, when and any dietary need, then shortlist templates. The shortlist below is worked out by the rules from the catalog. In the chat, the AI picks 2–4 of them as options and adds its designer's notes.

7. Open questions

#QuestionProposal
Q1Fully custom cakes outside the catalog?Not in iteration 1. The assistant says what we can't do and offers the nearest templates. A "custom request" path straight to the baker could come later.
Q2Should option prices scale with size?Flat prices for now. Later: one price per size band (small, medium, large), because fondant on a 3-tier cake costs more than on a 6" round.
Q3Party portions or wedding portions?Sizes state party portions. For weddings, the assistant also shows the wedding-portion number (about 1.5 times as many, because the slices are thinner).
Q4How much to build for iteration 1?All six lines and the full option library, but about 8 templates (2 per main occasion), so the preview pictures stay manageable.
